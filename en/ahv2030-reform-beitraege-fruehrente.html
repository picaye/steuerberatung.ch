AHV2030 is a consultation draft – not one line of it is law yet

AHV2030 is the biggest social-security reform currently under discussion in Switzerland. The Federal Council wants to secure AHV/AVS (first-pillar) finances for 2030 to 2040 and adapt the insurance to social change: the baby-boom cohorts are retiring, skilled labour is scarce, and the 13th AHV pension will cost more than CHF 4 billion a year from December 2026.

Important before any figure in this article: AHV2030 is not enacted law. The Federal Council opened the consultation on 20 May 2026; it ran until 11 September 2026. Everything described here is a proposal of the consultation draft – parliament, a possible referendum and the voters can still change every detail. What the bill does not contain: any increase of the AHV reference age. The Federal Council explicitly refrains from raising it; an expert commission will examine models for later reforms.

The reform goes back to a motion of the National Council's social security and health committee demanding AHV stabilisation for 2030–2040. Three groups are most affected: the self-employed (contribution rate), owners of AGs and GmbHs (dividends), and anyone planning early retirement (second pillar).

Timeline: dispatch in the first half of 2027, entry into force 2031 at the earliest

The reform timetable is set out in the Confederation's official documents:

  • 20 May 2026: the Federal Council opens the consultation on AHV 2030.
  • 11 September 2026: the consultation deadline expires; cantons, parties and associations have submitted their positions.
  • First half of 2027: the Federal Council intends to adopt the dispatch (Botschaft) to parliament – as stated in its published objectives for 2027. The consultation results, the outcome of the VAT vote of 29 November 2026 and the updated financial outlook will feed into it.
  • Then: parliamentary debate; depending on the course of events a referendum is possible, with a popular vote likely in 2029 or 2030.
  • 2031 at the earliest: entry into force. The explanatory report calculates the financial effects of the measures from 2031; the Federal Social Insurance Office (FSIO/BSV) stresses that questions on entry into force and modalities cannot be answered definitively today.

For your planning this means: a window of several years remains – but if you run an AG/GmbH or consider early retirement, you should know the direction of travel, because restructuring takes time.

Self-employed: the AHV rate is to rise from 8.1 to 8.7 per cent

Today the self-employed pay a lower AHV rate on their earned income than employees: a maximum of 8.1 per cent (Art. 8 AHVG), whereas employees reach 8.7 per cent together with the employer share (4.35 each). For lower incomes a degressive scale applies to the self-employed: anyone earning less than CHF 60,500 a year pays progressively less, down to a minimum rate of 4.35 per cent. In both cases IV and EO contributions are added.

The consultation draft proposes to align the maximum rate for higher-income self-employed with that of employees – 8.7 instead of 8.1 per cent. The degressive scale is kept in principle but, per the draft, would only apply up to a ceiling of CHF 40,500 (today CHF 60,500). The Federal Council justifies this with equal treatment and competitive neutrality: according to the explanatory report, more than 70 per cent of the self-employed benefit from the scale today, and their income is charged at 7.2 per cent on average.

Example: a self-employed architect has a relevant income of CHF 90,000. Under current law her AHV contribution is 8.1 % = CHF 7,290 a year (IV and EO contributions are added). At the proposed 8.7 % it would be CHF 7,830 – an increase of CHF 540 a year. (Illustrative calculation using the rates named in the draft – nothing is decided.)

The flip side stressed by the Federal Council: higher contributions also mean higher relevant income and therefore tend to produce better pensions. Little changes for the self-employed on low incomes – the scale still protects them. Details on today's rates and minimum contribution are in our article on the AHV minimum contribution for the self-employed.

Owners of AGs and GmbHs: dividends above a 15 % return become contribution-liable

The second big element targets owner-shareholders. Dividends are in principle contribution-free today – anyone paying themselves a high dividend instead of salary saves AHV contributions. The Federal Council wants to fight this with a statutory threshold: in future a dividend would be deemed «excessive» where its return exceeds 15 per cent of the invested capital. The amount above that threshold would count as relevant salary and become contribution-liable.

The draft text (new Art. 5 para. 2ter AHVG) is more precise: what counts is the share of income from participation rights paid by the employer that exceeds 15 per cent of the tax value of those rights per year; the cantonal tax authorities determine the tax value. According to the explanatory report, the rule addresses employee-shareholders holding at least 10 per cent of the share capital.

Today the compensation funds intervene only case by case: they reclassify dividends as salary where the salary is unreasonably low and the dividend manifestly excessive – most recently confirmed by the Federal Supreme Court in ruling 9C_532/2025 on the look-through of holdings. The draft would turn this practice into a fixed, automatic threshold – without the fund having to prove a disproportion in the individual case. This was disputed in the consultation: business circles consider 15 per cent too low, arguing that SME equity demands a higher risk return.

Important: this too is a proposal, not law. Owner-managed AGs and GmbHs setting salary and dividends today continue to follow the applicable case law – the AHV2030 threshold would arrive in 2031 at the earliest.

Closing contribution gaps: daily allowances, second-pillar buy-ins and platforms

Beyond the two big levers the draft contains further contribution measures the Federal Council calls «closing contribution gaps»:

  • Sickness and accident daily allowances become contribution-liable. So far these allowances are exempt from AHV contributions – unlike those of unemployment, EO, IV or military insurance. That is to be aligned; according to the Federal Council this improves the later pensions of the persons concerned.
  • Second-pillar buy-ins: the self-employed would no longer be able to shelter voluntary buy-ins into occupational pension schemes from AHV contributions to today's extent – equal treatment with employees.
  • Reporting duty for platform operators: electronic platforms would have to report earned income, making contribution gaps from gig and side work visible.
  • Modernisation: improved data exchange on persons not in employment, simplified payroll administration by specialised third parties (payrolling), and adjustments to insurance subordination.

Relevant for employers: if daily allowances become contribution-liable, payroll accounting would in future also cover social-security contributions on sickness allowances – a point consultation participants judged controversially.

Early retirement in the second pillar: minimum age rises from 58 to 63

The most incisive point for employees concerns occupational pension provision: the minimum age for early retirement is to be raised step by step from today's 58 to 63 years. Exceptions are possible per the Federal Council notably in company restructurings or under collective labour agreements; an absolute floor of 60 years applies in any case.

People retiring early would remain insured in the second pillar and be able to transfer their vested benefits to a new pension institution. The concrete modalities and transition rules are open – the FSIO notes in its FAQ that questions on entry into force and the design of the increase cannot be answered today.

In practice the proposal means: anyone planning early retirement between 58 and 63 would in future need an exception (GAV, restructuring) or would have to bridge the gap to the earliest possible BVG drawdown themselves. That is exactly what the bridging benefits for older unemployed persons are for – who can claim them and what they pay is explained in the linked article. For most people concerned this is a planning topic with a lead time of years, not months.

Working beyond the reference age: higher tax-free allowance, deferral with no age cap

Instead of raising the retirement age, the Federal Council wants to make working up to and beyond the reference age more attractive. The draft contains three incentives:

  1. Higher allowance: people who keep working after the reference age today benefit from an allowance of CHF 16,800 a year on which no AHV contributions are levied. The draft raises it to CHF 22,680 and provides for regular indexation. This income would also count more strongly in the pension calculation.
  2. Deferral beyond 70: today the AHV pension can be deferred at most until the 70th birthday. This cap is to be abolished – deferral would be possible beyond it, with continued contributions to the AHV.
  3. New reduction/increase rates: the actuarial rates for early drawdown and deferral are to be set according to positive and negative incentive principles in future instead of purely by life expectancy.

In parallel the Federal Council wants to create the foundations for more flexible pension models, with a view to a future individualisation of the reference age. Financing: everything hinges on the vote of 29 November 2026

AHV2030 cannot be thought of without the 13th AHV pension. It is paid for the first time in December 2026 and costs more than CHF 4 billion a year initially, and more than CHF 5 billion later. On 19 June 2026 parliament decided to finance part of it via VAT: the standard rate is to rise by 0.4 percentage points from 8.1 to 8.5 per cent, the special rate for the hotel sector from 3.8 to 4.0 per cent; the reduced rate of 2.6 per cent stays unchanged. Since this requires a constitutional amendment, the people and the cantons vote on 29 November 2026; the increase could enter into force in early 2028 at the earliest. More in our article on the 13th AHV pension in December 2026.

The outcome of that vote determines how much additional financing AHV2030 must shoulder: if the 13th pension is secured long term, the Federal Council sees in principle no need for additional financing within AHV2030. If the voters reject it or parliament decides on a limited solution only, the draft sketches alternatives – e.g. a VAT increase of 0.7 percentage points or a combination of VAT and contribution-rate increases.

The contribution measures contained in the draft themselves are expected to bring the AHV additional revenue of around CHF 600 million a year by 2040. As a safeguard the Federal Council also proposes an intervention mechanism: if the AHV compensation fund is set to fall below 90 per cent of one year's expenditure within the next three years, the Federal Council would have to submit stabilisation measures to parliament within a year.

In short: what to take away now

  • Nothing is law. AHV2030 is a consultation draft (20 May – 11 Sep 2026); the dispatch follows in the first half of 2027, a popular vote is possible, entry into force is expected 2031 at the earliest.
  • Self-employed would pay 8.7 instead of 8.1 per cent AHV contributions on higher incomes per the draft; the degressive scale stays but with a lower ceiling.
  • Owners of AGs/GmbHs: dividends above a 15 % return on the invested capital (resp. tax value) are to count automatically as salary. The applicable practice from ruling 9C_532/2025 remains unchanged until then.
  • Early-retirement plans before 2035 need recalculating: the BVG minimum age is to rise step by step from 58 to 63 (floor 60, exceptions possible).
  • Working longer becomes more attractive: allowance CHF 16,800 → 22,680, pension deferral also beyond 70 – all subject to parliamentary debate.
  • The next hard date is the vote of 29 November 2026 on the VAT financing of the 13th AHV pension; its outcome changes AHV2030's financing needs. We will update this article after the vote.
