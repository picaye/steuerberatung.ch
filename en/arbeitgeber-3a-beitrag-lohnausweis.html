When the Employer Pays Into Pillar 3a: AHV Duty, Salary Certificate Item 7 and Tax

Published on 2026-11-13 · Last updated 2026-11-13 · Independent tax information for Switzerland.

Why the contribution runs through payroll

Some small firms want to help staff build retirement assets and simply transfer a contribution to the employee's pillar 3a account. What sounds like a free gift is remuneration in social-insurance terms: voluntary benefits the employer provides to the employee are income "connected with the employment relationship" and therefore part of the AHV-relevant wage (AHV/IV/EO) under Art. 5 para. 2 AHVG (AHV/IV information sheet 2.01). Only the regular second-pillar contributions owed under law or the pension-fund regulations are exempt (Art. 7 AHVV) — a payment into an individual 3a account is never mandatory, so it is always contribution-relevant wages.

The cost consequences are real: on the 2026 maximum of CHF 7'258 (with a pension fund), AHV/IV/EO contributions of 5.3 % apply, split equally between employer and employee, plus ALV contributions of 1.1 % each while annual wages stay below CHF 148'200 (BSV synoptic rate table, as of 1.1.2026). That is roughly CHF 929 in additional social-insurance contributions — before any employer accident-insurance premiums. The benefit is not free money; it runs through the payroll account.

Practical tip: Recognised forms are tied pension agreements with bank endowment institutions and tied pension policies with supervised insurers (BSV). If the employer steers contributions to a vehicle affiliated with the company, the cantonal tax administration examines recognition and deductibility case by case — clarify before the first transfer, not after.

Salary certificate: item 7, not a deduction

The joint ESTV/SSK guide for filling in the salary certificate (form 11) is unambiguous: item 7 lists "all contributions made by the employer for its employee to recognised forms of tied self-provision (pillar 3a)" — whether deducted from wages or paid directly to the pension institution. The employer must not reduce the gross salary by these amounts; they are part of total gross income (item 8).

The second step runs through the pension institution: the bank foundation or insurer certifies the year's contributions to the insured person on form 21 (EDP). Without that certificate, the tax office grants no deduction — even if the money demonstrably reached the 3a account. For payroll this means the same contribution appears twice: as an item-7 entry on the salary certificate and separately on the institution's form 21.

Practical tip: Payroll teams working with ELM/Swissdec software should map employer pillar 3a contributions to item 7 in the wage master data. Booking them as a deduction (item 10) creates a divergence against the 3a certificate — and cantonal tax administrations reconcile that automatically.

Tax effect: zero today, reduced on withdrawal

The mechanics: the contribution is added as taxable income (item 7) but is fully deductible up to the maximum as a recognised pension contribution (Art. 33a DBG). The immediate tax effect of the employer's pillar 3a contribution is therefore close to zero — the capital is taxed only on withdrawal, at the separately computed reduced lump-sum rate. That deferral is the actual advantage over a cash bonus.

Worked example (this website's ESTV-calibrated tax engine, rounded): Andrea M., 38, single, lives in the city of Zurich, gross salary CHF 85'000, enrolled in a pension fund, pays the pillar 3a maximum of CHF 7'258 every year. Her employer is considering an additional benefit of CHF 7'258 at year-end.

  • Option A — cash bonus: taxable income effectively rises by CHF 7'258. Total tax 2026 (federal, cantonal, city of Zurich): CHF 10'139 instead of CHF 8'809 — an extra CHF 1'330 (18.3 % of the bonus).
  • Option B — employer pays CHF 7'258 directly into her 3a: the item-7 add-back and the pension deduction offset — Andrea pays CHF 0 in extra tax today and still achieves the same retirement provision as in option A.

Both options trigger identical AHV/IV/EO and ALV contributions (the amount is part of the AHV-relevant wage either way). The CHF 1'330 saved in Zurich is purely a tax deferral — withdrawal later incurs the reduced capital-withdrawal tax (staggering withdrawals over years lowers the bill; see the linked article below).

The maximum-amount trap — and a checklist for both sides

The annual maximum — 2026: CHF 7'258 with a pension fund, CHF 36'288 (20 % of AHV-relevant income at most) without — is an absolute ceiling per person and year, and employer contributions count fully against it. If the employee already exhausts the maximum personally, the employer's payment creates no extra deduction room: it is simply added as fully taxable wages, social-insurance contributions still apply — and the money is blocked on top. In that constellation the plain cash bonus is the better benefit; the 3a route only works when the deduction is actually available.

Checklist for employers: document the benefit in writing (one-off or regular, eligible group); declare it as salary-certificate item 7; bill the AHV-relevant wage correctly, including the ALV ceiling of CHF 148'200; pay only into recognised pension vehicles (form 21 must be clean); clarify cantonal practice on affiliated providers before launch.

Checklist for employees: Is the contribution shown under item 7 of your salary certificate? Do you hold the institution's form 21 covering the full amount including the employer's payment? Is the pension deduction entered in your tax return, and is the maximum (own plus employer contributions) respected? If in doubt, run the pillar 3a calculator — and for large amounts, engage a vetted specialist.
