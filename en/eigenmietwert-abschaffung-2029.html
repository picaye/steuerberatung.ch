Imputed Rental Value Abolished: What Swiss Homeowners Should Still Use Before End-2028

Published on 2026-09-19 · Independent tax information for Switzerland.

Contents:
  1. What has been decided: imputed rental value ends on 1 January 2029
  2. Until end-2028, the old system applies — with full deductions
  3. New from 2029: first-time buyer deduction for debt interest
  4. What this means for your mortgage and your planning
  5. Compensation: cantonal property tax on second homes
  6. In short

What has been decided: imputed rental value ends on 1 January 2029

On 28 September 2025, Swiss voters approved the reform of the taxation of home ownership by 57.7% of the vote. On 1 April 2026, the Federal Council brought the reform into force as of 1 January 2029 (FDFA/FST press release of 1 April 2026). From that date, the imputed rental value (Eigenmietwert) on owner-occupied homes is abolished at federal, cantonal and municipal level.

The system change goes further than just the imputed value: the Confederation also drops the deduction for maintenance costs (including value-preserving renovations) and for interest on debts secured on owner-occupied property. For rented or leased property, the maintenance deduction remains, and debt stays on a prorated basis — in proportion to the value of the rental properties relative to total assets.

At the federal level, deductions for energy-saving and environmental investments are also scrapped. Cantons may keep these deductions on a temporary basis — whether and for how long is up to each canton, whose legislatures are currently adapting their laws.

Practical tip: Mark 31 December 2028 in your calendar: until that date, the old system with full deductions applies. Any deduction potential you want to use must be realised in the 2026–2028 tax periods.

Until end-2028, the old system applies — with full deductions

During the transition period to 31 December 2028, you must continue to declare the imputed rental value as income. In return, you keep deducting maintenance costs, value-preserving renovations and mortgage interest on your owner-occupied home in full. Nothing changes in your 2026, 2027 or 2028 tax returns for now.

For many owners, it pays to review whether larger maintenance works — façade, roof, heating, windows — that are due anyway can be pulled forward into 2027 or 2028. From 2029 there will no longer be any tax deduction for such spending: the cost would be purely private, with no federal tax offset.

A blanket "renovate everything quickly" approach is not automatically wise, though: whether pulling works forward pays off depends on your income, your marginal tax rate and the scope of the project. Run the numbers over the affected tax years before signing contracts — and note that trades capacity may get more expensive towards the end of the transition, as many owners plan to renovate at the same time.

Practical tip: From now on, keep complete records of all property costs. In a final transition-year renovation, the clean split between value-preserving (deductible) and value-adding (not deductible) decides every franc.

New from 2029: first-time buyer deduction for debt interest

Anyone acquiring a home in Switzerland for the first time (for own use) from 2029 onward receives the new first-time buyer deduction for debt interest. Under the law brought into force for 1 January 2029, the maximum deduction in the first year after acquisition is CHF 10,000 for married couples and CHF 5,000 for all other taxpayers. The cap then falls by 10% per year — CHF 9,000 in year two, CHF 8,000 in year three — over ten years in total (FDFA fact sheet on the new debt-interest rules, Art. 33a FATA).

Important: the deduction is tied to the first acquisition of an owner-occupied home and runs from the purchase date, not from the reform's entry into force. Someone who bought their first home in 2025 will therefore start 2029 part-way through the ten-year tapering schedule.

For all other owner-occupiers the rule from 2029 is: no more deductible mortgage interest. That fundamentally changes mortgage strategy — today's tax-efficient high-debt structure becomes tomorrow's pure interest-cost trap.

Practical tip: Buying your first home soon? Time the financing start with 2029 in mind: the first-time buyer deduction runs from acquisition, and the full interest deduction on owner-occupied homes ends with 2028 anyway.

What this means for your mortgage and your planning

Under today's system, the interest deduction reduces the tax burden — paying down the mortgage often does not pay. From 2029 the logic reverses: every outstanding franc of mortgage on your home costs interest with no tax offset (beyond the first-time buyer or rental-business exceptions). That is exactly why experts warn against hasty moves: amortising the mortgage in full before 2029 forfeits the full deductions in the transition years and pays down debt "tax-unoptimised".

A sensible roadmap splits the remaining years into three stages. In 2026/2027, identify which maintenance and renovation phases are coming up and demonstrably pull major value-preserving works into the period through 2028. In 2028, compute your post-2029 situation (imputed value gone, deductions gone, wealth tax unchanged) and derive the amortisation strategy from it. From 2029, the new world applies — no imputed value, lower but predictable costs.

Cantons and municipalities are adapting their laws; open interpretation questions are being handled by a working group of the FST and cantonal tax administrations through a consultation process. Reliable cantonal figures on new rates and possible cantonal special deductions do not yet exist — rely only on your canton's official publications until cantonal parliaments have decided.

Practical tip: Have your individual transition plan (renovation timing, mortgage, amortisation) modelled by a tax professional before you commit big money in 2027/2028. We are happy to match you with a vetted partner in your canton.

Compensation: cantonal property tax on second homes

Alongside the reform, voters approved a constitutional provision allowing cantons to introduce a special property tax on predominantly self-used second homes, to compensate for lost revenue. Whether a canton will levy this tax, at what rate and from when, is not yet settled — cantons are currently drafting their rules for entry into force in 2029.

For holiday-flat and second-home owners this means: plan conservatively. The old balance between imputed value on one side and deductions on the other disappears; a new object-based tax may be added. Anyone considering selling a second home or moving it into the rental market should follow the legislative debate in the relevant canton before taking binding steps.

Practical tip: Watch the cantonal legislative process — the final parliamentary votes in 2027/2028 will make the real 2029 burden on your second home foreseeable.

In short

The imputed rental value is abolished as of 1 January 2029 — along with deductions for maintenance and debt interest on owner-occupied homes and, at federal level, energy and environmental deductions. Until 31 December 2028 the old system stands: declare the imputed value, use all deductions. First-time buyers get a temporary interest deduction (CHF 10,000 / 5,000, tapering over ten years). Cantonal details — energy deductions, second-home tax — are still being fixed; check your canton's official sources before finally adjusting your financing strategy.
