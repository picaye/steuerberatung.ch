Inheritance and gift tax: who pays in Switzerland – and who does not

Published on 2026-09-15 · Independent tax information for Switzerland.

  2. Spouses and children: usually tax-free
  3. Gifts: same rates – and a 5-year trap

The key point first: it stays cantonal

Switzerland has no federal inheritance or gift tax. Both taxes are levied exclusively by the cantons (in some cases additionally by municipalities) – with very different rates and allowances. The JUSO "Future Initiative", which wanted to introduce a federal inheritance tax of 50% on assets above CHF 50 million, was clearly rejected on 30 November 2025 with 78.3% voting No. So nothing changes in the cantonal system.

Taxable is generally the heir or the recipient of the gift – not the estate as a whole. Decisive is the last place of residence of the deceased or the donor: that is where the tax is due, regardless of where the heirs live. For real estate, the location of the property applies.

Practical tip: anyone expecting an inheritance or gift should first check the donor's canton of residence – the differences between cantons are enormous (0% to over 50% for distant relatives).

Spouses and children: usually tax-free

Good news for families: surviving spouses and registered partners are exempt from inheritance and gift tax in all cantons (one exception concerns Solothurn with the estate tax).

Direct descendants (children, grandchildren) are likewise tax-free in most cantons – exceptions: Appenzell Innerrhoden, Lucerne (where about half the municipalities tax descendants), Neuchâtel, Solothurn (partly) and Vaud. The more distant the relationship, the higher the rate: siblings pay considerably more than children, non-relatives the most.

Special case: Schwyz and Obwalden levy neither inheritance nor gift tax – Lucerne, Schwyz and Obwalden also levy no gift tax. Moving residence shortly before a gift is not a reliable strategy, however: several cantons tax gifts retroactively if the donor moves away or dies shortly afterwards.

Gifts: same rates – and a 5-year trap

In most cantons gifts are taxed at the same rate as inheritances – the only difference is the timing (during lifetime instead of on death). What matters is the aggregation rule: repeated gifts to the same person are added together in most cantons over a period of five years before death – and can thus move into higher progression bands. Giving in instalments therefore does not automatically save tax.

For gifts to children, also note the obligation to equalise (Swiss Civil Code): generous transfers to one child may have to be offset against the siblings if no other provision is made. This prevents inheritance disputes – provided it is documented correctly.

Practical tip: record every gift in writing (gift agreement, date, amount) and inform the other heirs. This saves expensive disputes later – and makes the tax return easier, since gifts must be reported in many cantons.

Allowances and rates: a cantonal patchwork

Every canton has its own allowances and progressive rates. For orientation (inheritance tax, examples):

  • Zurich: descendants 2–6% from a CHF 200,000 allowance; siblings 6–18%; other heirs 12–36%.
  • Zug: non-relatives 4–8% – one of the lowest rates in Switzerland.
  • Vaud: siblings up to around 15%, other heirs up to roughly 32–50% depending on the municipal share.
  • Schwyz/Obwalden: 0% – no inheritance or gift tax.

Important: these figures are points of reference – exact rates change and often also depend on the municipality. What is decisive is always the tax law of the canton of residence at the time of death or the gift.

Plan instead of being surprised: 5 steps

  1. Take inventory: list assets (property, accounts, securities, crypto – see crypto taxes), determine the canton of residence.
  2. Check the rate: who inherits how much – and what does it cost in the canton of residence? It gets expensive for patchwork families, unmarried partners and non-relatives (often 20–50%).
  3. Give early: with clear agreements, respecting the 5-year aggregation and the obligation to equalise.
  4. Settle business succession: company assets are favoured in many cantons – but only if succession is planned in time.
  5. Update will and inheritance contract: since the 2023 revision of inheritance law, compulsory portions are smaller – giving more room for planning that many do not yet use.
