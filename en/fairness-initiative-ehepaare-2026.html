What the fairness initiative on 29 November demands

On 29 November 2026, Swiss voters and cantons decide on the popular initiative “Yes to fair federal taxes for married couples – end discrimination against marriage!”. Backed by the Die Mitte party, it was filed on 27 March 2024 with 102,051 signatures; the Federal Chancellery declared it valid on 16 April 2024 with 101,382 valid signatures.

The substance: Article 128 of the Federal Constitution would newly state that for federal direct tax the income of married couples is aggregated, and that the law must ensure married couples are not disadvantaged versus other taxpayers – freezing today's joint assessment in the Constitution.

Key point: the initiative covers the federal direct tax only – cantons and communes are not directly regulated. The Federal Council and Parliament recommend rejection (final votes 19 June 2026: National Council 102:97, Council of States 24:20); there is no counter-proposal. Adoption requires a double majority – people and cantons.

Practical tip: a constitutional initiative needs the canton majority too, so small swings in populous cantons such as Zürich or Bern can decide the outcome – expect a close campaign.

Worked example: how the marriage penalty works today

Under today's joint assessment, spouses' incomes are added and taxed on the married schedule. Exact numbers from our calculator (2026 tax year, verified to the franc against the official FTA calculator): Dora and Emil, married, no children, taxable income CHF 80,000 each (combined CHF 160,000).

  • Joint assessment as a married couple: CHF 6,680 federal tax
  • Comparison – two unmarried taxpayers at CHF 80,000 each: 2 × CHF 1,378 = CHF 2,756
  • Federal-level penalty of being married: CHF 3,924 per year

The same mechanics work in reverse as a marriage bonus: a single-earner couple with CHF 90,000 taxable income pays CHF 1,365 federal tax jointly, while a single person on the same income pays CHF 2,024 – marriage saves CHF 659. The FTA estimates roughly 650,000 couples each benefit from or are penalised by the system at federal level.

Practical tip: the figures above isolate the schedule effect on taxable income; dual-earner and child deductions soften the real burden slightly. Whether your own marriage is penalised depends on income split, canton and deductions – run it through the calculator before drawing conclusions.

The system change is already approved: individual taxation

The marriage penalty is not a given: on 8 March 2026 voters approved the Federal Act on individual taxation with 54.23%. Eventually every person – including spouses – files an individual return on the same schedule. At federal level the switch takes effect no later than 1 January 2032 under the Federal Council's timetable; cantons must follow.

That is precisely the conflict: the fairness initiative would entrench joint assessment of married couples in the Constitution, directly contradicting the already-approved system change. A Yes would force the legislator back to couple-based taxation and bury individual taxation at federal level.

Practical tip: until individual taxation takes effect, married couples remain jointly assessed – the transition years 2027–2031 still run under today's rules. If you suspect a marriage penalty, plan accordingly today instead of waiting for the political transition.

What a Yes would change – deadline, fallback, consequences

The initiative does not prescribe how joint assessment must look – splitting models, multiple schedules and deductions remain the legislator's choice. But the transitional provision (draft Art. 197 sec. 15 Cst.) is concrete: if implementing law is not in force within three years of acceptance, the Federal Council must legislate by ordinance – including an alternative assessment calculated on the schedules and deductions for unmarried persons, with the lower of the two amounts levied.

The Federal Council estimates revenue losses of about CHF 700 million to CHF 1.4 billion per year in the ordinance scenario (2025 tax year, current schedules); roughly 78.8% would hit the Confederation and 21.2% the cantons. A No, by contrast, leaves the already-decided path to individual taxation untouched – no new constitutional norm.

Practical tip: steuerberatung.ch provides facts, not a voting recommendation. Either way, model your current burden now – because what changes in 2027 depends on the ballot.

Cantonal differences: the marriage penalty by canton

The initiative changes only federal tax – yet the total marriage penalty varies sharply by canton, because cantons and communes design couple taxation differently (splitting, family and spouse deductions, own schedules). Practical tip: relocating solely because of the marriage penalty is rarely wise – model canton, commune, housing costs and all deductions together first. Individual taxation will largely flatten this cantonal patchwork.

What couples should do regardless of the vote

Yes or No: until at least 2031, married couples remain jointly assessed at federal level. The proven levers keep working – and usually beat any constitutional clause: exhaust pillar 3a contributions for both spouses (including buy-ins where possible: pillar 3a buy-in; compute limits in the 3a calculator), bundle deductions, concentrate property maintenance in the right year.

For tax purposes the marital status as of the end of the tax period generally applies: couples planning a wedding should model the tax consequences of their first joint year – similar incomes create the largest penalty, while very unequal incomes may gain a bonus.

Practical tip: don't wait for the election fever: the site's tax checklist and saving tips show the levers that work in either system in 2026/27. For complex situations (property, self-employment), a professional review pays off.
