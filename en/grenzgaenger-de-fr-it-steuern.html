When you count as a cross-border commuter for tax purposes

For tax purposes, you are a cross-border commuter only if every condition is met: residence abroad (DE, FR or IT), employer and place of work in Switzerland, and daily return to your home – and that return must be reasonable. In cantonal practice, reasonable means: a driving distance of at most 100 kilometres by car or at most 1.5 hours by public transport.

Note: the cross-border commuter permit (ID G) is something else – it does not establish tax cross-border commuter status. Anyone who does not return home daily is an international weekly resident and is taxed at source under the regular Swiss tariffs.

Germany: 4.5 % source tax – but only with Gre-1

For genuine German cross-border commuters, Switzerland levies source tax as a flat rate of 4.5 % of the gross salary (Art. 15a DTA CH-DE). But this does not apply automatically: you must give your employer the certificate of residence, form Gre-1, issued by your German tax office. Without Gre-1, the employer settles accounts under the regular tariff – often considerably more.

Gre-1 is valid for one calendar year at a time and must be submitted to the employer anew every year; after the first year, the tax office usually issues it automatically. You need a separate form for each employer.

The 60-non-return-days limit

If you fail to return home for professional reasons (business trips, on-call duty, further training) on more than 60 days in the calendar year, you lose the cross-border commuter tariff. Your Swiss income is recalculated under the regular tariffs and reassessed. The employer must certify the professionally motivated non-return days using form Gre-3 – in the canton of Zurich, for example, in writing, separately per employer, and filed with the cantonal tax office no later than 31 March of the following year (the deadline varies by canton). Overnight stays for private reasons do not count.

Germany: tax return by 31 July

Even if Switzerland has collected 4.5 %: in Germany you are taxed on your worldwide income and must declare your cross-border commuter income in your income tax return. The general filing deadline for the 2025 German income tax return ends on 31 July 2026; later if you use tax representation (wage tax assistance association, tax adviser). The Swiss source tax is credited in Germany so that no double taxation remains.

France and Italy: completely different rules

France: The 1983 cross-border commuter agreement applies only to places of work in the cantons of BE, BL, BS, JU, NE, SO, VD and VS. Outside this zone – for example Geneva – the regular DTA applies, with the regular Swiss source-tax tariff and taxation in France with a credit; the details depend on the canton of work.

Italy: Under the revised agreement (in force since 17 July 2023, applicable from 2024), «new» cross-border commuters – those who started work after 17 July 2023 – may be taxed in Switzerland at up to 80 % of the regular source-tax amount; Italy credits this tax. For commuters employed before the cut-off date, a transitional rule applies until the end of 2033. Home office is possible for up to 25 % of working hours without jeopardising the status.

The most common mistakes

  • Is the G permit the same as tax status? No – what decides the matter is daily return and reasonableness.
  • Forgetting Gre-1 → regular tariff instead of 4.5 %, a tedious correction.
  • Non-return days documented incorrectly → Gre-3 not filed in time, reassessment under the regular tariff.
  • «Forms 130/140/150 with the AHV»? They do not exist – the AHV (social security) is never responsible for source tax; the certificates run through the employer and the cantonal tax offices.

In short

  1. 4.5 % Swiss source tax for genuine German cross-border commuters – only with form Gre-1 at the employer.
  2. More than 60 professional non-return days (Gre-3) → regular tariff, reassessment.
  3. German side: Anlage N-GRE (German annex N-GRE), filing deadline for the 2025 return on 31 July 2026.
  4. FR: 1983 agreement only in 8 border cantons; IT: new regime from 2024 (up to 80 % Swiss source tax, credit, transition until 2033).
