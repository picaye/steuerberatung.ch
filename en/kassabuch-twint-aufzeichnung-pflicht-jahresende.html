Year-end 2026: the cash register decides whether the books hold

Swiss VAT is a self-assessed tax: the business declares and pays the tax itself, and the Federal Tax Administration (FTA) reviews what was declared in audits it is empowered to carry out under Art. 78 of the VAT Act. The system relies on trust in the company's records – and that trust has one precondition: complete, contemporaneous entries backed by documents.

By the 2026 year-end close at the latest, it becomes clear whether the accounting holds. For cash-intensive businesses – restaurants, retail, kiosks, hairdressers, bakeries, take-aways – the cashbook is at the centre: cash receipts, TWINT and card payments converge there every day. This guide sets out what the VAT Act, the Code of Obligations and FTA practice require: from daily recording through retention and turnover reconciliation to the deadlines that apply around the 2026 financial statements.

Who must keep which books: two thresholds, two laws

Two different thresholds, two laws – do not confuse them:

  • Commercial-law duty to keep accounts (CO): sole proprietorships and partnerships keep full accounts once they achieve revenue of at least CHF 500'000 in the financial year (Art. 957 para. 1 no. 1 CO). Below that, it suffices to record income, expenditure and the financial position – the so-called simplified accounts (Art. 957 para. 2 CO); the principles of proper accounting apply by analogy (para. 3).
  • Direct taxes: anyone keeping simplified accounts files, with the tax return, statements of income and expenditure, of the financial position and of private withdrawals and contributions (Art. 125 para. 2 let. b of the Federal Act on Direct Federal Tax, DBG).
  • VAT (VAT Act): anyone VAT-registered – as a rule from CHF 100'000 of annual turnover (Art. 10 para. 2 let. a VAT Act) – must keep business books and records in accordance with commercial-law principles, regardless of the CO threshold (Art. 70 para. 1 VAT Act). The FTA may impose further recording duties where indispensable for proper tax collection.

In practice: a small take-away with CHF 300'000 of revenue needs no balance sheet under the CO – but if it is VAT-registered, its records must withstand a VAT audit. The daily cashbook is the foundation of that.

The daily cashbook: content, form, cash count

Swiss law does not prescribe a specific till or cashbook system. What matters is a record capable of serving as evidence: complete, contemporaneous, comprehensible and not reconstructed after the fact. Each cashbook row should contain:

  • the date (ideally with consecutive numbering per business day),
  • receipts separated by payment type – cash, TWINT, debit/credit card,
  • payments out (cash disbursements such as petty purchases or topping up the float),
  • the running balance and the closing cash figure for the day.

For cash-intensive businesses, practice also requires the daily cash count: at closing, the actual cash is counted, compared with the cashbook's expected balance, and every difference is recorded and explained. Every entry needs a supporting document – receipt, till roll (Z-bon), invoice, TWINT or terminal extract. Recording must be chronological and systematic: the accounting journal captures all posted transactions in chronological order (Art. 1 para. 2 let. b of the Business Records Ordinance, GeBüV), and the accounts must be complete, truthful and systematic (Art. 957a para. 2 CO).

Practical tip: anyone who "assembles" the cashbook at month-end from memory or bank statements is producing an after-the-fact record – precisely the constellation that carries no evidentiary weight in an audit.

TWINT and cards: electronic receipts are not a side note

A widespread misconception: "TWINT and cards are not cash, so they do not belong in the cashbook." True, electronic receipts do not enter the cash balance – but they are every bit as subject to the recording obligation and form part of the consideration that must be captured without gaps and declared. For day-to-day operations that means:

  • Keep a separate column or subsidiary record for each payment type: cash, TWINT, card.
  • Capture the daily totals from the TWINT app and the card terminal – just as you do for cash.
  • Keep the payment processors' settlement reports (daily and monthly closes) as accounting documents and reconcile them with the recorded amounts.
  • Post the gross receipts: processor and acquirer fees are an expense, not a reduction of turnover.

In the turnover reconciliation the electronic receipts must remain traceable – the ordinance expressly lists "other incoming payments not included in the reported operating revenue" (Art. 128 para. 2 let. f of the VAT Ordinance, MWSTV). Wherever the TWINT flow is invisible in the accounting, an audit will sooner or later surface a difference you must explain.

Practical tip: if a private TWINT account is used for business takings, transfer those receipts into the business records without gaps – mixed private/business payment flows are a classic audit finding.

Retention: ten years – twenty for real estate

The retention periods at a glance:

  • VAT: business books, supporting documents, business papers and other records must be properly kept until the tax claim is absolutely time-barred – as a rule ten years after the end of the tax period (Art. 70 para. 2 in conjunction with Art. 42 para. 6 VAT Act).
  • Commercial law: business books and accounting documents – as well as the annual and audit reports – must be kept for ten years; the period starts at the end of the financial year (Art. 958f para. 1 CO).
  • Real estate: business documents needed to calculate input-tax relief on immovable property and own use must be kept for twenty years (Art. 70 para. 3 VAT Act).

Electronic retention is permitted: books and documents may be kept on paper, electronically or in a comparable manner, provided consistency with the underlying transactions is guaranteed and they can be made readable again at any time (Art. 958f para. 3 CO; on proper data processing, Art. 2 para. 2 GeBüV). TWINT and card settlement reports usually arrive electronically only – store them so that they remain readable and reproducible ten years from now.

Turnover reconciliation: what it is and what belongs in it

The turnover reconciliation (Umsatzabstimmung) shows how the declaration for the tax period – taking account of the different tax rates or of the net/flat rates – is brought into line with the annual accounts (Art. 128 para. 2 MWSTV). The ordinance lists, in particular:

  • the operating revenue shown in the annual accounts;
  • income posted to expense accounts (expense reductions);
  • intra-group offsets not included in operating revenue;
  • sales of fixed assets, advance payments and the other incoming payments not included in the reported operating revenue;
  • benefits in kind, revenue reductions and debtor losses;
  • closing entries such as accruals, provisions and internal reclassifications that are not turnover-relevant.

In a VAT audit the FTA requires the turnover reconciliation for each year – starting from the balances of the relevant revenue accounts, taking account of accruals and special items, against figure 200 of the settlement forms; businesses accounting under the effective method additionally provide an input-tax reconciliation. For everyday work: reconcile monthly or at least quarterly. Anyone who only looks at December once the year has closed is hunting for a needle in a haystack.

Deadlines: day 180 and the 240-day finalisation

Two deadlines interlock around the year-end close:

  • Day 180 (Art. 72 para. 1 VAT Act): if, when preparing the annual accounts, you discover defects in your tax settlements, you must correct them no later than in the settlement for the accounting period into which the 180th day after the end of the financial year falls. For the 2026 financial year (calendar year), day 180 is 29 June 2027 – with quarterly accounting, the correction must therefore appear in the Q2 2027 settlement at the latest.
  • Finalisation after 240 days (FTA practice): if no correcting settlement reaches the FTA within 240 days of the end of the financial year – 28 August 2027 for 2026 – the FTA assumes the submitted VAT settlements are complete and correct and that the tax period is finalised.

Corrections are made in the form prescribed by the FTA (Art. 72 para. 3 VAT Act) using the official form "Jahresabstimmung (Berichtigungsabrechnung nach Art. 72 MWSTG)"; only the differences from the settlements submitted so far are declared. If the comparison with the annual accounts reveals no defects, no correcting settlement is required. And note: recognised defects must also be corrected after the fact outside the year-end close, as long as the tax claims are neither res judicata nor time-barred (Art. 72 para. 2 VAT Act).

Money is at stake: if the correction happens only at finalisation via the correcting settlement, default interest is owed – it runs from the due date of the tax period concerned (in general 60 days after the end of the tax period, respectively 28 February of the following year). Correcting early saves interest.

Worked example: a restaurant day and a reconciliation month (illustrative)

Example (illustrative): a restaurant on a Tuesday in September 2026 – one row in the cashbook:

  • Opening cash balance: CHF 300.00
  • Cash receipts (food, drinks): CHF 1'240.00
  • TWINT receipts: CHF 860.00
  • Card payments: CHF 1'900.00
  • Cash payment out (petty cash to supplier): CHF 150.00
  • Expected cash balance: 300.00 + 1'240.00 − 150.00 = CHF 1'390.00
  • Cash count at closing (actual): CHF 1'390.00 – difference: CHF 0.00
  • Total daily turnover across all payment types: CHF 4'000.00

Monthly turnover reconciliation (illustrative), September 2026:

  • Operating revenue per the revenue accounts: CHF 97'500.00
  • less revenue reductions (discounts, rebates): CHF 1'000.00
  • less sale of a fixed asset (old espresso machine, not in operating revenue): CHF 500.00
  • = turnover declared in the VAT settlement, figure 200: CHF 96'000.00 – reconciled, the difference is explained.

Both examples use invented, purely illustrative amounts; they explain the method and are not advice for any individual case.

Gaps, retroactive books – and what happens then

Where records do not exist or are incomplete, or where the reported results obviously do not match the true state of affairs, the FTA assesses the tax claim at its discretion (Ermessenseinschätzung, Art. 79 para. 1 VAT Act). For cash-intensive businesses, the Federal Supreme Court's long-standing case law spells out what a cashbook is worth: a cash record reconstructed after the fact, and entries without contemporaneous supporting documents, carry no evidentiary weight; in the worst case the accounts as a whole are rejected and turnover is estimated. Such estimates regularly go against the business – with uplifts, back taxes and default interest.

Gaps also reach beyond VAT into the direct taxes: the annual accounts are the basis for determining profit – if the cashbook does not hold, the tax authorities will not take the declared profit at face value either. The point is not formalism but substance: can every franc of declared turnover be traced from the supporting document through the settlement into the tax return – and back? A daily cashbook with properly filed TWINT and card documents answers that question long before any audit.

Checklist to the 2026 year-end close

  • Daily: complete the cashbook the same day – with columns for cash, TWINT and card, a daily total and a cash count at closing (record any difference).
  • Documents: one supporting document per entry; keep terminal, TWINT and processor settlement reports readable for at least ten years (real-estate documents: twenty years).
  • Monthly: reconcile the revenue accounts with the VAT settlements (turnover reconciliation) – not only at year-end; under the effective method, reconcile input tax too.
  • 2026 year-end: correct defects in the 2026 settlements by 29 June 2027 at the latest (day 180) via a correcting settlement in the then-open accounting period; from 28 August 2027 the FTA treats the tax period as finalised.
  • Save interest: correcting early, before finalisation, avoids default interest running from the original due date.
