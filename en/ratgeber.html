Tax Guide 2026

The most important topics of the Swiss tax return 2026 – from deadlines and deductions to withholding tax and home ownership. Independent, easy to understand, up to date.

Tax deadlines 2026: by when must the tax return be filed?

The filing deadline for the 2026 tax return depends on the date the form was delivered: in principle, 31 March 2027 applies as the final deadline; in some cantons, however, it ends already 30 days after receipt of the tax form. Since the cantons deliver the forms at different times, the actual deadline varies from canton to canton – in practice it usually falls between mid-January and the end of March. Anyone who misses the deadline risks late-filing surcharges and a higher assessment.

Important: the deadline keeps running during holidays, illness or a move. Extensions are only possible in justified exceptional cases and must be requested from the cantonal tax office before the deadline expires. The safest approach is to file the tax return a few weeks before the official deadline – electronically via the cantonal portals or by post.

Our recommendation: note the date on which you received the form and count the 30 days from there. If in doubt, our free initial consultation helps: we check your deadline and, if needed, put you in touch with a vetted partner in your canton.

Deductions 2026: these costs lower your tax bill

The most important deductible costs in the 2026 tax return are social security contributions (AHV/IV/ALV) – these are deducted in full. Also deductible are contributions to the third pillar (pillar 3a), up to a maximum of CHF 7'258 per year for employed persons with a pension fund; for employed persons without a pension fund, up to 20 % of earned income, maximum CHF 36'288 (as of 2026) – as well as the health insurance premiums of the entire family.

In addition, occupational expenses can be deducted if they are work-related: commuting costs (under certain conditions), work clothing, continuing education costs, account maintenance fees for the salary account and – for the self-employed – all business expenses. Maintenance contributions for children, donations to charitable organisations and pension contributions for the second pillar are also deductible.

Practical tip: collect receipts throughout the year – from the health insurance bill to the certificate of further training. If a receipt is missing, the deduction is often rejected in the assessment. A vetted partner reviews your return to ensure that all permissible deductions have actually been claimed.

Cross-border commuters: tax liability in Germany, France or Italy

Cross-border commuters are persons who live in Switzerland but work in a neighbouring country (Germany, France, Italy) – or vice versa. Special rules apply to them: the deciding factor is the place of residence: whoever lives in Switzerland and works abroad is fully liable to tax in Switzerland – the tax paid in the country of employment is credited. Whoever lives abroad and works in Switzerland generally pays only source tax (payroll tax) in Switzerland – the final settlement takes place in the country of residence. Double taxation agreements (DTAs) prevent the same income from being taxed twice.

Whoever lives in Switzerland (e.g. Zurich, Schaffhausen) and works in Germany remains fully liable to tax in Switzerland and credits the German payroll tax as foreign tax. For work in France or Italy, the respective DTAs with Switzerland apply; in certain constellations the tax is withheld in the country of employment and credited in Switzerland. The settlement is handled via the forms for foreign income and the foreign tax certificate.

Cross-border commuter cases are complex and prone to errors – a single mistake can quickly cost several thousand francs. We connect you with specialised partners who know the DTAs with Germany, France and Italy and handle your situation correctly.

Self-employed: invoicing and handling VAT correctly

Self-employed persons and business owners issue invoices with the legally required invoice details (VAT Act): name and address of the supplier and the recipient, date, description and quantity of the service, price and – for taxable turnover – the VAT indication. If invoicing is not correct, the customer's input tax deduction may be at risk and your own bookkeeping becomes vulnerable.

Crucial is the VAT registration deadline: if your taxable turnover exceeds CHF 100'000 within 12 months, you must register with the federal VAT authority (ESTV) – within 30 days of exceeding the threshold. If registration is late, back payments and default interest threaten. Businesses with turnover below CHF 100'000, on the other hand, are not subject to VAT – but they can register voluntarily in order to claim the input tax deduction.

Also note: AHV/IV/ALV contributions are settled via the AHV compensation office, profit tax and income tax are determined via the profit and loss statement, and depreciation on fixed assets (e.g. computers, vehicles) must be documented correctly. A vetted partner supports you with invoicing, VAT registration and preparation of the annual accounts.

Withholding tax: 35 % retention and form 110

Withholding tax is a Swiss speciality: on certain investment income – above all account interest, dividends and bond interest – 35 % is retained. This retention is not a final tax: you get it back, provided you declare the income correctly in your tax return. Without correct declaration you lose the right to a refund.

The refund is made via form 110 (withholding tax return), which you submit together with your tax return. Important: the return must be filed within the statutory deadline – generally together with the tax return for the same assessment year. Note: withholding tax is levied only on income from Swiss sources – foreign accounts and securities portfolios are not affected.

Practical tip: check your bank statements and portfolio statements for the note "withholding tax 35 %". Enter the amounts in full on form 110 – many taxpayers overlook interest from secondary accounts or from securities portfolios. A vetted partner ensures that not a single franc of your refund is lost.

Home ownership: mortgage interest and imputed rental value

Whoever owns residential property can deduct mortgage interest in full from tax – for both the first and the second mortgage. Also to be considered is the imputed rental value: for owner-occupied property it is added as notional income; at the same time, mortgage interest is granted as a deduction. This offset can lead to a net tax relief with a high mortgage – however, the imputed rental value is set differently from canton to canton.

The imputed rental value is determined differently by each canton (often around 60–70 % of the market rent). What matters is the assessment of your canton of residence – it is stated in the cantonal tax form or communicated by the tax office. The difference between the imputed rental value and the actual interest costs determines whether home ownership has a net relieving or burdening effect for tax purposes.

Additionally deductible are property maintenance costs, building insurance premiums and – depending on the canton – property tax; for rented-out property, on the other hand, rental income must be declared as income. Errors in the split between the owner-occupied and the rented portion are common. We connect you with partners who correctly reflect your mortgage structure in the tax return.
