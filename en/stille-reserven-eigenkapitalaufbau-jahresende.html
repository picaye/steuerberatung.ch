What hidden reserves are — and who really benefits

A hidden reserve exists whenever a business carries assets lower than their true value: machinery written down to zero, inventory held at a discount, receivables reduced by documented loss rates, identifiable risks booked as provisions. The gap between book value and real value has not yet been taxed — and that is exactly the lever. Once revealed or realised, the reserve is taxed as ordinary income later on.

Self-employed individuals and sole-proprietor owners enjoy a double benefit. First, progressive-rate arbitrage: forming the reserve reduces profit in a strong year at the marginal rate, while the later realisation is often taxed in a weaker year at a lower average rate. Second, the money stays in the business — a booked provision is liquidity, not an expense. For AG and GmbH companies the effect is far smaller, because corporate profit is taxed almost proportionally; there, building equity pays mainly for risk reasons.

Practical tip: hidden reserves are not an end-of-year trick but balance-sheet management with substance. Collect evidence on inventory and debtor risks all year and you can book well-founded entries on 31.12.2026 instead of guessing.

What you may form on 31 December — and what tax authorities add back

The benchmark is prudent commercial judgement: depreciations, write-downs, provisions and losses are deductible insofar as they are required or sufficiently sized under commercial assessment (Art. 58 of the Direct Federal Tax Act; cantons and the Federal Supreme Court apply the same standard to the self-employed in substance). What year-end legitimately allows:

  • Depreciation of movable business assets down to near zero where condition and useful life support it;
  • Write-downs on inventory and receivables based on documented experience rates or specific identifiable risks;
  • Provisions for concrete risks — warranty obligations, pending litigation, contracts expected below cost;
  • Deferred income for advance payments and deposits not yet earned in 2027.

Not recognised — and added back at assessment, with reassessment risk in cases of concealment — are flat-risk reserves without a tangible trigger, private spending booked as a business expense, and arbitrary profit smoothing across years. Business real estate has a special regime: book values may be kept low; when the property is transferred to private wealth, what is generally taxed are the "reintroduced depreciation" amounts, while the appreciation gain stays deferred until sale (FTA Circular No. 28).

Practical tip: keep a one-page justification per item (multi-year experience rates, order list, court order). Cantonal schedules such as the "statement of provisions and write-downs" demand exactly that — and well-documented reserves survive any assessment.

Worked example: CHF 25,000 less profit — what it saves in Zurich

A self-employed IT specialist in the city of Zurich, single, no children, books a 2026 profit of CHF 140,000. On 31.12. he forms a provision for a pending performance dispute (CHF 9,000), writes down receivables by a documented experience rate (CHF 6,000) and fully depreciates a machine (CHF 10,000). Total: CHF 25,000 less taxable profit — the 2026 book result falls to CHF 115,000.

Realisation: ordinary in a weak year — or privileged under Art. 37b

Absent a special trigger, a revealed reserve is taxed ordinarily in the year of realisation. Plan the release into a weak business year: in the Zurich example the specialist earns only CHF 40,000 in 2028; adding the CHF 25,000 reserve raises the bill by CHF 4,377 — a burden of just 17.5% instead of 30.2%. Compared with the original deduction that is a net gain of roughly CHF 3,170, plus the interest benefit of deferral.

The privileged route is far stronger: whoever definitively ceases self-employment after the age of 55 or due to disability taxes the hidden reserves realised in the last two business years separately from all other income — at one-fifth of the ordinary tariff, subject to a minimum rate of 2% (Art. 37b DBG; details in the liquidation-gain ordinance LGBV). Voluntary pension-fund buy-ins in the liquidation year and the year before are first deducted from ordinary income and otherwise reduce the privileged gain. In the event of death, heirs and legatees who do not continue the business may claim the same relief — the assessment must happen at the latest five calendar years after the year of death.

Two traps: the privilege is granted once — a later return to self-employment enjoys it no more. And only a "minor" continuing activity without staff or premises is permitted, capped at the occupational-pension entry threshold.

In short

  • Hidden reserves = carrying assets below real value; forming them cuts 2026 profit, realising them taxes it later.
  • Allowed: depreciation, write-downs and provisions under prudent commercial judgement (Art. 58 DBG) — anything arbitrary is added back.
  • Zurich example: a CHF 25,000 reserve saves CHF 7,550 (30.2%); in Zug CHF 5,191, in Bern CHF 8,689.
  • Release in a weak year; on definitive cessation after 55 or due to disability: separate tax at 1/5 of the tariff, minimum 2% (Art. 37b DBG).
  • Sequence: pillar 3a (CHF 7,258) and pension buy-ins first, hidden reserves after — run the numbers.
